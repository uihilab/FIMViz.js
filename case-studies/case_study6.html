Case Study 6 — A snowpack that emptied in three days

A temporal flood-generation study run entirely in the browser: multi-dimensional ingest, axis selection, per-step reduction and derived surfaces, with no server-side compute. Protocol: CASE_STUDY_PROTOCOL.md §0.

The question. Between 7 and 16 March 2019 a bomb cyclone crossed the central Plains and fell as rain onto a ripe snowpack over frozen ground. Eastern Nebraska flooded catastrophically. Rain on snow is not simply a lot of rain: the snowpack is a reservoir that empties on the same day the rain arrives, so the water reaching the soil is rain plus melt, against soil that is already at capacity. How much of the water was melt rather than rain, and how long did the soil stay saturated afterwards?

This study reports a water balance and a duration, not a flood extent. The National Water Model's terrain-routing fields — the ones that would carry a modelled inundation depth — are unpopulated in the v2.1 retrospective (sfcheadsubrt is a constant 0 and zwattablrt a constant 2 across the entire record), so there is no modelled water surface in this dataset to threshold. What there is, and what is used here, is the forcing, the snowpack, and the soil column.

Fig. 6.1The reservoir emptying, and the column filling

Snow water equivalent SNEQV, mm

The reservoir. Depth of water held as snow, if the pack melted where it lies. On 10 March it covers almost the whole domain; by the 14th it is gone. This is not snow depth — it is the water inside the snow, which is what reaches the soil.

Top-layer soil moisture SOIL_M, m³/m³

The receiver. Water per unit volume in the top 10 cm. Around 0.45 the pores are full and further input runs off rather than infiltrating. Watch it brighten as the left panel empties — the two are the same water, three hours apart.

—

The controls. The slider moves both panels through the — three-hourly steps of 10–17 March; play runs it at roughly four steps a second. Each panel is one lazy Dataset, and moving the slider calls ds.select(coord) — only the step you are looking at is ever decoded, which is why an 8-day series costs no more to open than a single map. Dragging never queues work: a decode already in flight suppresses the next request rather than stacking behind it. All five maps on this page share one view, so panning or zooming any of them moves the rest.

The strip plots the domain mean of both variables across the whole window — snow water equivalent in blue against soil moisture in amber, each scaled to its own range so the shapes can be compared rather than the magnitudes. The red rule marks where the slider is. It is drawn from the same single pass that produced Fig. 6.2, not from a second reading of the data.

Fig. 6.2What the event left behind

Four surfaces, all from the single pass over the series that also drew the strip above — the page walks the 57 steps once and accumulates everything on the way through, rather than re-reading the data per product. Each answers a different question about the same event: how much water the snowpack released, how wet the ground got, how long it stayed that way, and when it happened. The last is the one that separates cause from coincidence.

Cumulative melt ACSNOM, mm over the window

How much water the snowpack gave up, per cell, summed over 10–17 March. This is the headline number made spatial: the bright areas delivered a second rainfall's worth of water to ground that was already full. Summed from positive increments because the source accumulator resets mid-record — differencing its endpoints returns a negative total.

Peak soil moisture max over the window

The wettest each cell ever got. Identical to what ds.reduce("max", { axis: 0 }) returns — the page computes it both ways and reports the largest disagreement between them in the table below, because a hand-rolled reduction that nothing checks is an assertion rather than a result.

Saturation duration hours ≥ 0.40 m³/m³

How long the ground stayed full — steps at or above 0.40 m³/m³, times three hours. These are floors, not residence times. The window opens with much of the domain already wet and closes with much of it still wet, so a cell reading the full 168 h was saturated before 10 March and probably after the 17th too. The table counts how many.

Time of peak hours from 10 March 00Z

When each cell reached its own maximum. The most diagnostic of the four: cells peaking early responded to melt, cells peaking late to the rain that followed. A domain that peaked all at once would point to rainfall alone; the spread here is what implicates the snowpack.

§Method notes


    
  
  • Melt is summed, not differenced. ACSNOM is a running accumulator and it
      resets inside the record, so taking the difference of its endpoints returns a negative
      melt total. The page accumulates positive per-cell increments instead, and reports the number
      of reset cell-steps it saw. Per cell, not per domain mean: cells reset at different
      steps, and a domain-mean series smears one cell's reset across the whole field.
    • 
  
  • A projected grid silently collapses, and the engine's own advice does not fix it. NWM is
      Lambert Conformal Conic and its coordinate arrays are in metres. extractGrid
      samples using those arrays while the engine supplies a bbox in degrees, so every sample point
      falls outside the source range and nearest-neighbour clamps them all to one cell — yielding a
      spatially uniform grid with no error raised, whose values still vary over time so it
      reads as working code. The engine rejects the metre bbox with a good message ending "pass one
      explicitly: grid: { bbox: [...] }" — but that relabels the extent without
      changing the sampling. The mirrors here rewrite x/y into degrees at build time; that is the
      actual fix, and it is why grid.bbox below is a label rather than a rescue.
    • 
  
  • Chunking, not volume, decides what a page can do. The source chunks time in blocks of
      224 — right for "one cell, forty years", wrong for "one map, next map". Every
      select() decompresses the whole chunk to return one slice, measured at ~776 ms per
      step, so a full walk of the hourly series took 8.7 minutes headless. The page mirrors re-chunk
      to [1, ny, nx], one step per chunk, which is the single change that makes a slider
      viable. Same bytes, same engine, three orders of magnitude of interactive difference.
    • 
  
  • Live Zarr stores cannot be read. The reader is Zarr v2 in a zip;
      scan() against a store URL range-requests the directory and S3 answers 416. The
      bucket is CORS-open and serves ranges, so the limit is the reader, not the host — but it means
      every Zarr study is a mirrored study.
    • 
  
  • .zarr.zip needs its format stated. detectFormat() reads the
      last extension, and .zip routes to the shapefile reader — the failure is
      "not a valid shapefile", which names the wrong subsystem entirely.
    • 
  
  • Rain is integrated at 3 h from an hourly rate. The page samples RAINRATE
      every third hour to share the 3-hourly axis of the other three variables, so its total is a
      sampled integral, not an hourly one. The headless run integrates all 672 hourly steps; the two
      totals appear side by side in the table so the size of that shortcut is visible rather than
      assumed.
    • 
  
  • The window is the event, not the record. The page carries 10–17 March. The analysis
      mirrors carry the full 28-day chunk and the headless run reports over all of it — including the
      recession to zero snow water equivalent on 22 March, which this page ends too early to show.
    •
