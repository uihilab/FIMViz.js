Case Study 9 — March 2019 on the Missouri, from gridMET

Three gridMET year files (daily precipitation, minimum and maximum temperature at 1/24° over the conterminous US) read as NetCDF4 in the browser, byte for byte as the agency serves them. March 2019 is split into rain and snow per cell and day, and summarized over the Missouri River basin above Hermann, MO, and the Platte River basin above Louisville, NE. Protocol: CASE_STUDY_PROTOCOL.md §0 and §2.

The question. The mid-March 2019 storm produced record flooding on the Platte and lower Missouri. How much of the basin's March precipitation fell as rain, and how much of the 13–17 March rain fell on cells that had received snow in the preceding twelve days?

§Data and method

Weather. gridMET (Abatzoglou, 2013) is a daily surface-meteorology dataset for the conterminous United States on a 1/24° grid (about 4 km), from 1979 to the present, released under CC0. It blends the spatial detail of the PRISM climate grids with the daily timing and additional variables of the NLDAS-2 regional reanalysis by climatologically aided interpolation (Drought.gov, n.d.). This study reads three of its variables for 2019: daily precipitation total (pr, mm) and daily minimum and maximum air temperature (tmmn, tmmx, K). Each variable ships as one NetCDF4 file per year holding 365 daily grids; the page reads the three files as the agency serves them, packed as 16-bit integers with a scale factor and an offset, which the reader unpacks. Table 9.0 lists what the builder recorded about each file.

Basins. The USGS Network Linked Data Index returns the drainage area above a streamgage as a polygon. This study uses two: the Missouri River at Hermann, Missouri (USGS 06934500), which drains a large part of the northern Great Plains, and the Platte River at Louisville, Nebraska (USGS 06805500), a tributary basin inside it that includes the Nebraska rivers that flooded in March 2019. Both polygons carry hundreds of holes (areas that do not drain to the gage), which the page masks out.

The phase rule. A day's precipitation in a cell counts as rain when that day's mean of tmmn and tmmx is above a threshold, 0 °C by default, and as snow otherwise. It is the simplest rule a daily dataset supports: it has no knowledge of when in the day precipitation fell or of the elevation range inside a 4 km cell. Three windows frame the event: 1–12 March before the storm, 13–17 March during it, and the whole month. The storm dates are this study's framing, not a field in the data. Basin figures are means over the cells whose centers fall inside a basin, so they are depths in millimeters, not volumes.

A day's precipitation counts as rain in a cell when that day's mean temperature there is above the threshold, and as snow otherwise: tavg.reclassify() gives an open mask, pr.combine([mask], { op: "min" }) keeps the rain, and a window total is a running combine([day], { op: "sum" }). Snow is pr.difference(rain) over the window. The rule is one daily threshold per 1/24° cell; it models neither snow within a day nor snowpack, melt, or frozen ground.

snowBefore.reclassify(v => v >= min ? OPEN : 0) opens the cells that received the minimum snowfall, and a min combine keeps the event rain there. Snowfall is a proxy for snow on the ground: gridMET carries no snow water equivalent, so snow that melted before 13 March still counts.
